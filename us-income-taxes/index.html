        A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement account that lets you invest pre- or post-tax dollars with tax advantages. Money grows tax-free while it's in the account.

        For 2026, the IRS elective deferral limit used on the slider below is $23,500.

        The core tradeoff between the two types

        Traditional (pre-tax): You contribute before taxes, lowering your taxable income now. You pay taxes when you withdraw in retirement. Better if you expect to be in a lower tax bracket later.

        Roth (post-tax): You contribute after taxes, so no upfront benefit. But withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free. Better if you expect to be in a higher bracket later — or just want tax-free income in retirement.

        Investment losses

        If your investment losses for the year are bigger than your gains, up to $3,000 of the net loss comes off your ordinary income, lowering your tax like a pre-tax 401(k) contribution.

        Larger losses carry forward, so you can keep deducting $3,000 a year until they're used up.

        About this model

        Educational visualization using simplified rules for the selected tax year. Federal figures come from IRS Rev. Proc. 2024-40 (2025, as amended by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act) and Rev. Proc. 2025-32 (2026), plus the IRS bracket tables. The Child Tax Credit ($2,200 per child) includes the refundable portion (up to $1,700 per child, 15% of earnings over $2,500) and the income phase-out. The Saver’s Credit is simplified. The Earned Income Tax Credit and itemized deductions are not modeled.

        State tax: Utah (flat rate, 4.5% in 2025 and 4.45% in 2026, + taxpayer credit phase-out), California (brackets + 1% mental health surtax + exemption credits), Idaho (5.3% flat with small zero bracket), New York (state brackets; lower rates in 2026), Oregon (brackets + federal tax subtraction + exemption credit), Arizona (2.5% flat), Nevada/Texas/Washington (no state income tax). Some 2026 state amounts not yet published use 2025 values; the app notes which when 2026 is selected.

        The model covers wage income. The optional capital loss deduction subtracts $3,000 of net capital losses from ordinary income for federal and state tax; capital gains themselves are not modeled. Includes employee Social Security and Medicare only, not the employer share.
